Turn 16 · the categorical card

The most common column type, and the one asked to be four things

Eight of Titanic's twelve columns are categorical, and they are not one kind of thing. Sex has two levels, Embarked three, Cabin 147 mostly-empty ones, Name 891 unique. One card face serves all four, so it is tuned for none of them — and twelve of its statistics are computed for a case that only sometimes exists.

Credit where due The NaN length bug, the three-buttons-reading-2 chooser and the _unknown_cell pattern are all fixed, and that dash-that-says-why is better than what I specified. This turn is about what the card is for, not those.

Three of the twelve — Entropy, Rare levels, Top 5 coverage — describe how a distribution spreads across levels. On a two-level column there is nothing to spread. On a fully-unique column there is no distribution. They are meaningful for exactly one of the four kinds.

One face per kind
16a

Route on level count, as the datetime card routes on chunks

The report already has this pattern in three places: missing values routes on chunk count, correlations on numeric column count, the datetime year chart on span. Do the same here — the level count is known before anything is drawn, and it decides which statistics can be true.

PRO

Every statistic shown is one that can be true for that column. The even-split rule beside each bar is what makes a share judgeable — 72.4% against a 33.3% mark says "dominated by one port" without the reader computing anything, and it is the same device as the flat-calendar rule on the datetime card. Nothing new is computed: the level counts, the coverage and the extremes all already exist.

CON

Three faces means a reader scrolling eight categorical columns meets three different layouts, and the boundary cases are arbitrary — a 12-level column and a 22-level one are the same kind of thing but land either side of a line. It is also three code paths where there is one, and the stat row stops being comparable down the page.

16b

Or keep one face and suppress per row

The conservative alternative. One layout, twelve slots, and a statistic that cannot be true for this column is simply not rendered — the row closes up rather than printing a dash. Same rule as _unknown_cell, one step further: absent instead of unknown.

PRO

One layout and one code path, so eight categorical columns read alike and the grid stays comparable. The suppression rule is per statistic rather than per kind, which means no arbitrary level boundary and no argument about where 12 levels belongs.

CON

A varying number of slots makes the stat row a different height per column, so the page loses its rhythm in a subtler way than three layouts would. And it fixes only the false statistics — Cabin still gets a bar chart of 147 levels where a sentence would serve, because that is a chart decision and this is a stat-row rule.

What I'd do

16b, then the even-split rule from 16a on top of it. That gets the two real wins — no statistic that cannot be true, and a share the reader can judge — without three layouts or a level boundary to argue about. 16a's many-level face is worth taking anyway as a chart decision rather than a face decision: Cabin's 147 levels are not a bar chart at any width.

The three code findings are independent of both and each about ten minutes. The --data-4 one I would take first, because it is a violation of a rule I wrote and shipped, and it is invisible until someone looks at a below-threshold pair on paper.
